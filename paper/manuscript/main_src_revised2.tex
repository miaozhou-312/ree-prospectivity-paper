\abstract{Carbonatite-hosted rare earth element (REE) prospectivity models must learn from a few tens of occurrences and no confirmed barren ground. We test whether a semi-supervised Deviation Network (DevNet) outperforms Random Forest (RF), tuned tree ensembles, two positive--unlabelled (PU) learners and a supervised twin network in the Superior Province, Canada (15 occurrences), with the Cordilleran Orogen and Grenville Province as contrasts. Inputs combine potential-field, lithosphere and structural layers with H3-grid lithology, era and contact classes, distances to generalised regional geology and neighbourhood statistics. In Superior, DevNet's average precision (AP) is 0.45 under random cross-validation and 0.42--0.25 under 50--200~km spatially blocked cross-validation, against 0.27 and 0.19--0.15 for RF. It exceeds RF, tuned RF and gradient boosting, bagging-PU and nnPU in all six repeats under every protocol (nominal $p \leq 0.04$; Holm-adjusted $p = 0.07$ against RF). The supervised twin is level with DevNet; the deviation loss adds skill only without point-level map classes, clearly so with three training positives ($\Delta$AP $+0.07$, $p < 0.001$). One mapped alkalic-intrusion class, covering 6 of 15 occurrences and 0.5\% of the background, carries over a third of DevNet's blocked AP. DevNet's map is more stable than RF's when occurrences are left out, and an untrained Fe--REE--Nb resource ranks at its 97th percentile. In Cordillera and Grenville no model transfers between blocks; splitting augmented S2 child cells between training and test sets yields test AUC near 1 regardless of skill.}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Rare earth elements (REEs) are critical to low-carbon energy, electronics and defence technologies, yet economic concentrations are geologically uncommon \citep{Wang2020}. Most REE resources are hosted by carbonatites and associated alkaline intrusions, whose distinctive but partly overlapping geophysical and geochemical signatures make them natural targets for data-driven mineral prospectivity mapping (MPM; \citealp{Verplanck2014,Simandl2018}). In Canada, however, confirmed carbonatite-hosted REE occurrences are few and spatially clustered (Fig.~\ref{fig:study}): after near-duplicates are merged, a single geological province holds between about ten and thirty of them.

Two properties of this problem undermine standard supervised classification. First, positives are extremely scarce: mineralisation is a rare event \citep{Xiong2018}, known occurrences are only a partial and biased sample of the mineral system \citep{Coolbaugh2007,Parsa2021,Zuo2021}, and under $k$-fold cross-validation each fold holds only a handful of them, so any estimate is dominated by which occurrences happen to be held out. Second, there are no true negatives. Ground without a recorded occurrence is unexplored rather than barren, and designating it negative is an arbitrary modelling choice whose outcome depends on which background points are drawn \citep{Zuo2020}. Together these properties motivate positive--unlabelled (PU) and semi-supervised anomaly-detection formulations, in which mineralised sites are treated as rare anomalies relative to a large, mostly barren but unlabelled population \citep{Chen2019,Xiong2016,Xiong2021,Bekker2020,Luo2026}.

The Deviation Network (DevNet; \citealp{Pang2019}) is a semi-supervised deep anomaly detector that learns an anomaly score from a few labelled anomalies and a large unlabelled pool, pulling the scores of unlabelled data towards a Gaussian reference while pushing labelled anomalies a fixed margin above it. \citet{Luo2026} adapted it to MPM (DEEP-SEAM), encoding geology as distances to mapped units and adding neighbourhood statistics of the evidence layers. Whether such a model outperforms well-tuned discriminative baselines when positives number in the low tens has not been tested under evaluation designs that control for the specific ways in which small, clustered label sets inflate scores, nor has it been separated from the simpler explanation that any smooth neural scorer would do as well. The closest prior study, a national-scale model of Canadian carbonatite-hosted REE $\pm$ Nb deposits \citep{Parsa2024}, reported test AUCs of 0.91--0.96 with random forests and one-dimensional convolutional networks. Its labels were augmented by replacing each occupied S2 grid cell with its four child cells, its negatives were drawn from the ground least similar to the positives, and it was evaluated by stratified random 70/30 splits of the augmented labels. A follow-up study recommended synthetic labels generated by generative adversarial networks (GANs) for the same commodity \citep{Parsa2025}. Each of these design choices can make the held-out test easier than prospecting new ground.

This study benchmarks DevNet for regional carbonatite-hosted REE prospectivity in the Superior Province of the Canadian Shield, the province in which labels are scarcest relative to area. The Cordilleran Orogen and Grenville Province are modelled with the identical pipeline as contrasting cases. We ask: (i) does DevNet outperform tree-based and PU learners once every model sees the same data and folds, the tree-based learners are tuned and uncertainty is quantified? (ii) does any advantage survive spatially blocked validation that mimics prospecting unexplored ground? (iii) how much of it belongs to the semi-supervised deviation loss rather than to the network and its inputs, and when does that loss matter? (iv) how much of the skill comes from geological map classes that mark known complexes? and (v) can synthetic positives compensate for label scarcity, and how much of a high reported AUC can the validation design itself produce? We close by interpreting the models with SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP), using a rank-based rule for the direction of each feature's effect, and by checking the Superior maps against an untrained REE resource and the mapped Kapuskasing Structural Zone.

\section{Geological setting and data}\label{sec:data}

\subsection{Carbonatite-hosted REE systems and their geophysical expression}\label{sec:mineral-system}

Carbonatites occur predominantly in Precambrian cratonic areas, and individual provinces record repeated emplacement over hundreds of millions of years, implying long-lived, episodically reactivated trans-lithospheric pathways \citep{Woolley2012}. A component of their REE endowment is attributed to subcontinental lithospheric mantle refertilised by REE- and CO$_2$-rich metasomatic fluids \citep{Hou2023,Yaxley2021}. Each element of this source--transport--trap system has a mappable geophysical proxy (Fig.~\ref{fig:mineral-system}). Depth to the lithosphere--asthenosphere boundary (LAB) indexes the thickness and edge geometry of the cratonic root that hosts the fertile mantle source, which exerts first-order control on magmatic ore systems \citep{Griffin2013,Groves2021,Hoggard2020}; Moho depth indexes crustal thickness; and proximity to faults and to the loci of maximum horizontal gravity and magnetic gradient (``worms''; \citealp{Hornby1999}) proxies the structures that channel ascending melts. At the emplacement site, dense and magnetic alkaline--carbonatite complexes produce positive gravity anomalies and intense circular to annular magnetic anomalies \citep{Thomas2016,Drenth2014}. Grey-level co-occurrence matrix (GLCM) textures summarise the local two-dimensional heterogeneity produced by such concentric patterns without assuming a target diameter \citep{Cooper2005,HallBeyer2017}, unlike explicit radial-symmetry detectors tuned to ring-complex size \citep{Aranha2021}.

\subsection{Study areas}\label{sec:areas}

The three study areas are the Superior Province, the Grenville Province and the Cordilleran Orogen as outlined on the Geological Map of Canada \citep[Map 1860A;][]{Wheeler1996}. Each is modelled independently because carbonatite-hosted occurrences of the Phanerozoic, accreted Cordillera are geophysically distinct from those of the Canadian Shield \citep{Parsa2024}, so a single cross-terrane model would not be expected to transfer.

The Superior Province is the world's largest Archean craton. Carbonatite--alkaline magmatism in the Canadian Shield clusters into episodes at about 2.68~Ga, 1.90--1.88~Ga and 1.16--1.08~Ga \citep{Rukhlov2010}, and in the Superior Province many of the complexes are aligned along crustal-scale structures, most prominently the Kapuskasing Structural Zone \citep[KSZ;][]{Percival1994,Gittins1967}. The 15 Superior occurrences used here (Fig.~\ref{fig:study}b) comprise a cluster around the Montviel and Lac Shortt carbonatites in the eastern Abitibi region of Qu\'ebec (Deposit/Prospect and Showing class) and occurrences dispersed across the southern and western Superior Province, including Nemegosenda Lake, Lackner Lake, Kapuskasing, Martison Lake, Prairie Lake and Carb Lake. The Superior Province thus combines the fewest occurrences per unit area of the three provinces with a spatial structure, one tight cluster plus scattered singletons, in which a model can either learn the cluster or learn what the scattered occurrences have in common.

\subsection{Occurrence labels}\label{sec:labels}

Positive labels were compiled from the carbonatite-hosted records of the USGS global REE occurrence database \citep{Orris2018} and the occurrence table of \citet{Parsa2024}. Alkaline-hosted occurrences were excluded after a preliminary leave-block-out classification showed them to be geophysically separable from carbonatite-hosted ones. Records within 2~km of each other were merged by single linkage, so that drill holes or zones of one deposit, listed once or in both sources, count once; without this step near-identical points from one deposit fall into both training and validation folds. Merging and the data-coverage filter (Section~\ref{sec:domain}) reduced 21, 13 and 51 pooled records to 15 (Superior), 12 (Grenville) and 27 (Cordillera) positives. Each positive keeps the development class of \citet{Parsa2024}: Deposit/Prospect, Showing or Occurrence (Superior: 3, 4 and 8).

\subsection{Geophysical and structural evidence layers}\label{sec:layers}

Table~\ref{tab:layers} lists the evidence layers. Seventeen national rasters cover the trap and the deep-architecture components: Bouguer gravity and its derivatives and filtered versions, total magnetic intensity and its differential reduction to the pole with derivatives \citep{Jobin2017,Miles2016}, together with long-wavelength gravity and magnetics, LAB and Moho depth from the Tri-National Critical Minerals Mapping Initiative release \citep{McCafferty2023}. Eleven of the gravity and magnetic grids are processed products supplied by Getech Group plc; four of these are gridded versions of the public Natural Resources Canada compilations and seven are standard derivatives of them (Table~\ref{tab:layers}). Two line layers (ancient passive margins, \citealp{BradleyDC2008}; faults) and four point layers (shallow and deep gravity and magnetic worms; \citealp{McCafferty2023}) enter as normalised proximities. GLCM contrast, homogeneity, energy and correlation were computed in 9$\times$9-pixel windows on the Bouguer anomaly, its 40~km high-pass, the reduced-to-pole magnetics and the total magnetic intensity. Following \citet{Luo2026}, the mean and standard deviation of the same four grids within 10~km of each point were added as neighbourhood statistics. Together these give 47 geophysical and structural candidate features. Radiometric grids (K, eU, eTh) were considered but not used, because their coverage of the study areas is incomplete and uneven; whole-rock geochemistry is too sparse to give a value at every map cell. The province polygons of \citet{Wheeler1996} define the study areas only; no distance-to-province-boundary feature was used.

\subsection{Geology and the three feature sets}\label{sec:featuresets}

Geology enters from a hexagonal H3 grid (resolution 7, cells of about 5~km$^2$) attributed from the Geological Map of Canada \citep{Wheeler1996}. Each cell carries a major lithology class (for example Igneous Intrusive Alkalic or Metamorphic Gneiss Paragneiss), its dominant era, and flags for cells crossed by a lithological or an age (period) contact; each point takes the attributes of the cell that contains it. How this geology is encoded matters, because at the scale of a carbonatite complex a bedrock map can mark the target itself: in Superior, 6 of the 15 occurrences fall on cells mapped as alkalic intrusive rock, against 16 of the 3,000 evaluation-background points (0.5\%). We therefore compare three feature sets, each combining the geophysical and structural candidates with a different encoding of geology.

\textbf{Set 1 (grouped classes)}, the encoding of the original study, uses one-hot dummies for five era classes and four lithology groups: alkaline-favourable (alkalic intrusions, pegmatites and anorthosite), other igneous, high-grade metamorphic and sedimentary. It omits the neighbourhood statistics.

\textbf{Set 2 (no point-level map classes)} contains no class of the cell in which a point lies. Instead it measures distances (km) to six elements of a generalised map, from which every lithological unit smaller than 20 connected cells (about 100~km$^2$) and the alkalic-intrusion class altogether were removed, so that intrusions of carbonatite-complex size no longer exist on it: lithological contacts, age contacts, greenstone (extrusive) units, mafic--ultramafic intrusions, high-grade metamorphic units and sedimentary units \citep[cf.][]{Luo2026}.

\textbf{Set 3 (raw classes; main analysis)} adds to set 2 the raw lithology class of the cell as one-hot columns (a class gets its own column if at least 10 of the region's 5,000 training-background points fall in it; rarer classes share one), the raw era one-hot and the two contact flags.

Set 3 gives every model the most complete geological evidence, and an alkaline complex already shown on a map is legitimate evidence for an explorer, so it is the main analysis. Set 2 measures what the models achieve without point-level map classes, and set 1 links the results to the original study. After pruning (Section~\ref{sec:pruning}), set 3 has 48 features in Superior (24 geophysical and structural layers, 3 neighbourhood statistics, 2 regional distances, 15 lithology columns, 2 era columns and 2 contact flags), 55 in Cordillera and 58 in Grenville; set 2 has 29, 31 and 33, and set 1 has 35, 33 and 33.

\subsection{Sampleable domain and background}\label{sec:domain}

Rasters were clipped to each study-area outline buffered outward by one GLCM window radius (8~km), so that windows near the boundary read real data, and sampling and prediction were restricted to the domain where every layer holds data and every texture window is computable. This domain covers 98.6\% of the Superior, 97.2\% of the Grenville and 73.5\% of the Cordilleran outline; one Cordilleran occurrence (Lempriere) falls outside it and was dropped. Background candidates are the nodes of a 0.1$^\circ$ grid inside the domain and more than 10~km from any known occurrence (19,371 in Superior). From these, two disjoint pools were drawn once per region at random: an evaluation background of 3,000 points and a training background of 5,000 points. Every model in this study is trained on the positives of its training fold plus the same 5,000 training-background points and scored on the held-out positives plus the same evaluation background. The prevalence in the scored pool is therefore 15/3,015 $\approx$ 0.5\% in Superior, which is the AP of a random ranking.

\subsection{Feature pruning}\label{sec:pruning}

The candidate features include many collinear geophysical derivatives. Within each region and feature set, features were clustered hierarchically on $1-|\rho_{\mathrm{S}}|$ (Spearman correlation, average linkage) and cut at $|\rho_{\mathrm{S}}| = 0.7$, keeping one representative per cluster chosen by lowest missing rate and then highest variance \citep{Dormann2013}. The rule never sees labels and therefore cannot leak information across folds.

\subsection{Models}\label{sec:models}

All models see the same rows and differ only in how they treat the training background or in their loss.

\textbf{Random Forest} (RF; \citealp{Breiman2001}) is the discriminative baseline: positives versus training background labelled 0, 300 trees, $\sqrt{p}$ features per split, balanced class weights, inside a pipeline that fits median imputation and standardisation on the training fold only. With roughly 12 training positives against 5,000 background points, most held-out points receive a predicted probability of exactly zero, so the pooled AUC depends on which trees happen to vote for a given held-out occurrence; changing only the RF random seed moved a single-split Superior AUC between 0.889 and 0.985 in the original feature set, while AP varied far less. We therefore vary the RF seed across folds so that this variance enters the reported intervals.

\textbf{DevNet} \citep{Pang2019} maps each input vector $\mathbf{x}$ to a scalar score $\phi(\mathbf{x})$ through one hidden ReLU layer with dropout. The deviation $d(\mathbf{x}) = (\phi(\mathbf{x}) - \mu_R)/\sigma_R$ is measured against the mean and standard deviation of a fixed reference sample of 50,000 standard-normal draws, and the loss
\begin{equation}
L = (1-y)\,|d(\mathbf{x})| + y\,\max\{0,\, a - d(\mathbf{x})\}
\end{equation}
pulls unlabelled background ($y=0$) towards the reference and pushes positives ($y=1$) beyond a margin $a = 5$, the value of \citet{Pang2019} (Appendix~\ref{app:sensitivity}, Table~\ref{tab:margin}). Network capacity was set a priori by positive count (64 hidden units, dropout 0.3, weight decay $10^{-4}$ in Cordillera; 32, 0.4 and $10^{-3}$ in Superior and Grenville) rather than tuned, because a search over a few tens of positives fits noise. Training uses Adam \citep{Kingma2014} with weight decay, cosine learning-rate annealing over 300 steps and gradient clipping, and each fold score is the rank average of five restarts with different seeds.

Two design choices adapt DevNet to tabular evidence layers with very few positives. First, inputs are mapped to standard-normal marginals by a quantile transformation fitted on each training fold. Many evidence layers are strongly skewed (inverted distances concentrated near 0 or 1, long-tailed textures, 0/1 dummies), and unlike tree ensembles, which are invariant to monotone transformations, multilayer perceptrons are sensitive to such marginal distributions \citep{Gorishniy2021,Grinsztajn2022}. Second, each gradient step uses 16 positives (resampled with replacement) and 240 background rows. The deviation loss is meant to learn what the background looks like; with label-balanced batches of 16 + 16 rows, 300 steps would show each background row about once and each positive about 400 times, whereas background-heavy batches show each background row about 14 times while positive exposure is unchanged. Both choices were settled with diagnostic runs on feature set 1 (Appendix~\ref{app:sensitivity}); Section~\ref{sec:robust-methods} describes how the resulting selection optimism was measured.

\textbf{MLP-BCE} is DevNet's supervised twin: the same network, quantile-normal inputs, batches of 16 positives and 240 background rows, 300 steps and five-restart ensemble, but trained with binary cross-entropy with the background labelled 0. DevNet versus MLP-BCE therefore isolates the semi-supervised deviation loss from everything else.

\textbf{Bagging-PU} \citep{Mordelet2014} trains 200 fully grown decision trees, each on all training positives and an equal-sized bootstrap sample of the unlabelled background, and averages their outputs. It is the standard tree-based PU learner and the closest PU analogue of RF.

\textbf{nnPU} \citep{Kiryo2017} minimises the non-negative PU risk with the sigmoid loss, using exactly DevNet's network, quantile-normal inputs, optimiser, number of steps and five-restart ensemble, so that DevNet versus nnPU isolates the PU risk from the deviation loss. nnPU requires the class prior $\pi = P(y=1)$ in the unlabelled population; we use $\pi = 0.01$ (sensitivity in Appendix~\ref{app:full}, Table~\ref{tab:prior}). The calibration approach of \citet{Elkan2008} is not included because under its assumptions the calibrated output is a monotone transform of the positive-versus-background classifier, so its ranking, and every rank metric reported here, equals RF's.

\textbf{RF-tuned and HGB-tuned} give the tree-based baselines a design search of their own. Inside every outer training fold, an inner spatially blocked cross-validation (100~km blocks, three folds, training background purged from inner validation blocks) chooses the RF's minimum leaf size (1 or 10) and number of features per split ($\sqrt{p}$ or $p/3$), or the number of leaves (8 or 31) and minimum leaf size (5 or 40) of a histogram gradient-boosting classifier \citep{Pedregosa2011} with balanced class weights, 200 iterations, learning rate 0.05 and an L2 penalty of 1. The outer test fold is never used for the choice.

\subsection{Evaluation protocol}\label{sec:protocol}

With three or four positives per validation fold, per-fold AUC is unstable and can be undefined, so out-of-fold scores are pooled \citep{BradleyAP1997}: each held-out point is scored once per repeat, scores are converted to within-fold percentile ranks so that folds are comparable, and a single AUC and average precision (AP) are computed over all folds. Pooling carries a small known negative bias in AUC \citep{Airola2019,Parker2007}. We emphasise AP because, at 0.5\% prevalence, it reflects the practical task of concentrating occurrences into a small search area, whereas AUC is dominated by the bulk of easily rejected background \citep{Saito2015}. Exploration efficiency is also shown as capture-rate curves: the share of held-out occurrences ranked within a given top fraction of the scored pool \citep{Chung2003,Agterberg2005}. Because the evaluation background is a random sample of the domain, this fraction approximates search area. Because unexplored background may host undiscovered occurrences, all metrics are conservative.

\textbf{Random cross-validation.} Repeated stratified 5-fold cross-validation with six repeats gives six pooled AUC and AP values per model, reported as the median and the 2.5--97.5 percentile interval over repeats (labelled 95\% interval). All models use identical splits, so they can be compared pairwise.

\textbf{Spatially blocked cross-validation.} Random folds can hold out an occurrence a few kilometres from a training occurrence. Following \citet{Roberts2017} and \citet{Valavi2019}, labelled points were assigned to square blocks of 50, 100 or 200~km side in Canada Atlas Lambert (EPSG:3978), whole blocks were held out together using stratified group 5-fold splitting (fewer folds if fewer blocks contain positives), and every training-background point falling inside a held-out block was removed from training for that fold. Each of six repeats uses a random offset of the block grid.

\textbf{Paired comparisons.} For each pair of models we average each point's out-of-fold rank over the six repeats and draw 5,000 stratified bootstrap resamples of positives and background \citep{Efron1993}, reporting the difference in AP and AUC with its 95\% percentile interval, the one-sided proportion of resamples in which the difference is $\leq 0$ (denoted $p$), and the number of the six repeats in which the first model has the higher AP. With 12--27 positives these tests have little power. For the nine spatially blocked comparisons of DevNet with RF, bagging-PU and nnPU in Superior we also report $p$ values adjusted with Holm's procedure \citep{Holm1979}; MLP-BCE, a control rather than a competitor, is outside this family. Elsewhere the nominal $p$ values should be read as descriptive. Averaging ranks over repeats also acts as a small ensemble, which can interact with the quantity being compared; for the augmentation experiments, where this matters, we therefore report the paired per-repeat AP difference on identical folds instead.

\textbf{Geography-only baseline.} To separate geological signal from spatial memorisation, an RF was trained on the projected coordinates of each point only, with no evidence layer, under the same folds and both protocols. Its score measures how much of a protocol's reported skill can be obtained simply by remembering where the training occurrences lie. An L2-regularised logistic regression with balanced class weights serves as a smooth, essentially linear discriminative reference.

\subsection{Controls and robustness analyses}\label{sec:robust-methods}

\textbf{Label scarcity and background contamination.} The premise of PU and semi-supervised learning is that the unlabelled background contains undiscovered occurrences. In every outer training fold, all but $k$ training positives ($k = 8, 5, 3$; Superior folds hold about 12) were withheld in one of two ways: dropped, which leaves fewer labels, or hidden in the training background, which leaves fewer labels and a background that contains deposits. The same positives are withheld for every model, and held-out positives are always all real occurrences. The difference between the two conditions at equal $k$ is the cost of background contamination, which a semi-supervised loss should pay less of than a supervised one. This was run on feature set 1 with and without its geology dummies; the $k = 3$ condition was repeated for DevNet and MLP-BCE on sets 2 and 3.

\textbf{Nested design selection.} DevNet's input transformation and batch composition were settled with diagnostic runs on these three provinces, so its scores may carry selection optimism. To measure it, on feature set 1 the choice among the four combinations of z-scored or quantile-normal inputs and 16 or 240 background rows per step was made inside every outer training fold by the inner spatial cross-validation described above (two restarts per candidate), and the chosen design, refitted on the whole outer training fold, scored the outer test fold. The difference from the final DevNet is the optimism of having made the choice with the test folds in view.

\textbf{Per-occurrence analysis and map stability.} To see which occurrences carry the Superior results, each positive's out-of-fold percentile among the evaluation background was averaged over the six repeats and compared between the eastern-Abitibi cluster (the eight occurrences joined to Montviel by single linkage at 50~km) and the seven dispersed occurrences. Prediction--area curves \citep{YousefiCarranza2015} were computed from the same out-of-fold ranks, with the evaluation background as a proxy for area. To assess the stability of the maps, each Superior occurrence was left out in turn and the production models were refitted on the other 14; for every grid node we report the share of these 15 models that place it in the province's top 5\%.

\textbf{Province diagnostics.} To describe, independently of any model, why skill differs between provinces, each label set was characterised by its number of independent sites (occurrences merged by single linkage at 10~km), the share of occurrences that share a site with another and the median distance to the nearest other occurrence. The signal shared by distant occurrences was measured with a far-neighbour index: with features expressed as percentile ranks, it is, for each occurrence, the share of background points more than 100~km away that are closer to it in feature space than the most similar occurrence more than 100~km away. A value near 0 means that distant occurrences resemble one another more than they resemble almost any background. The median over occurrences, computed on the geophysical and structural features, is compared with the same statistic for 200 sets of background points drawn at random to pose as occurrences.

\subsection{Synthetic positives}\label{sec:augmentation}

To test whether synthetic positives can compensate for label scarcity, each training fold's real positives were augmented with $m \times n_{\mathrm{pos}}$ synthetic positives ($m = 1, 3, 10$) before RF and DevNet were retrained on feature set 1; the held-out positives were always real. Three generators were compared, all fitted in the standardised feature space of the training fold only: Gaussian jitter of real positives ($\sigma = 0.1$ standard deviations); SMOTE-style interpolation between a positive and one of its three nearest positive neighbours \citep{Chawla2002}; and a tabular Wasserstein GAN with gradient penalty \citep[WGAN-GP;][]{Goodfellow2014,Arjovsky2017,Gulrajani2017} with an eight-dimensional latent input, two hidden layers of 64 units in generator and critic, five critic updates per generator update, gradient-penalty weight 10 and 2,000 generator updates, trained on the fold's 12 positives. Synthetic geology dummies were snapped back to valid one-hot values. To see what the generators produce, each was also fitted to all 15 Superior positives and 150 samples were characterised by the distance to the nearest real positive divided by the median nearest-neighbour distance among real positives (a value near 0 indicates copying), by the share of samples whose nearest labelled neighbour is a positive rather than a background point, and by their DevNet score percentile.

\subsection{Validation-protocol audit}\label{sec:audit-methods}

To quantify how the validation design of \citet{Parsa2024} behaves on these data, we rebuilt it with the same S2 geometry library. Each occurrence's level-12 S2 cell (about 4.5~km$^2$) was replaced by its four level-13 child cells, and all features were extracted at the child-cell centres with the same pipeline. An equal number of negatives was drawn either from the 5\% of background candidates with the lowest mean cosine similarity to the augmented positives in min--max scaled feature space (as described by \citealp{Parsa2024}) or at random. Random forests of 1,000 trees on min--max scaled features were evaluated with 50 stratified 70/30 splits. Three split designs were compared: (i) child-level random splitting, as described in the original study; (ii) splitting with all children of one parent kept on the same side; and (iii) no augmentation, one label per occurrence. We report mean test AUC, its 2.5--97.5 percentile range over the 50 splits, and the share of test positives with a sibling in the training set. The audit uses the unpruned 48 candidates of feature set 1 and our layers rather than those of the original study, so it measures the sensitivity of the protocol, not the original study's numbers.

\subsection{Interpretation and mapping}\label{sec:shap}

Production models were fitted to all positives and the training background of each region. SHAP values \citep{Lundberg2017} were computed with TreeSHAP for RF \citep{Lundberg2020} and DeepSHAP for DevNet on an explain set of all positives plus evaluation-background points (200 in total), with 100 training-background points as DevNet's reference sample; DevNet's SHAP values refer to its quantile-transformed inputs, which leaves the rank-based directions defined below unaffected. DevNet SHAP values were averaged over the five ensemble members. Importance is mean $|$SHAP$|$. The \emph{direction} of a feature's effect is the Spearman correlation between the feature's value and its SHAP value over the explain set (for one-hot dummies, the sign of the mean SHAP difference between presence and absence), with $|\rho| < 0.1$ reported as no clear direction. We do not use the sign of the mean SHAP value, a tempting summary that reflects where the explained points lie relative to the explainer's baseline, not whether higher feature values raise the score: for RF with balanced class weights the TreeSHAP baseline is near 0.5 while nearly all explained points score near 0, so that rule would label every RF feature as ``higher $\rightarrow$ less prospective''. Prospectivity maps classify each model's scores on a 0.1$^\circ$ prediction grid into the top 1\%, 5\% and 10\%, because DevNet's anomaly score and RF's probability are not on a common scale. As an external check, the production models scored Clay-Howells, a drill-tested Fe--REE--Nb prospect with reported resources on the KSZ \citep{OGS2011} that is not among the training labels, at its recorded location.

\subsection{Model comparison under random cross-validation}\label{sec:res-random}

In Superior all models except nnPU rank held-out occurrences far above chance (Table~\ref{tab:benchmark}): AP is 0.22--0.45 against a random-ranking value of 0.005, and AUC is 0.97--0.98. DevNet has the highest median AP (0.445) and RF the highest AUC (0.983). DevNet's AP exceeds RF's, bagging-PU's and nnPU's in all six repeated splits, by 0.21--0.26 on repeat-averaged ranks ($p = 0.005$--0.024; Table~\ref{tab:bootstrap}). Its supervised twin MLP-BCE is level with it (AP 0.433; DevNet higher in three of six repeats), and on repeat-averaged ranks MLP-BCE is marginally ahead ($\Delta$AP $-0.034$, 95\% interval $[-0.086, -0.001]$). nnPU, which shares DevNet's network and inputs, reaches an AP of only 0.152 and an AUC of 0.838.

The contrasting regions behave differently. In Cordillera, MLP-BCE, bagging-PU and RF lead (AP 0.427--0.438) and DevNet trails (0.341; lower than RF in all six repeats, interval of the difference $[-0.228, +0.092]$). In Grenville all models are weak and RF leads (0.257), ahead of DevNet (0.125) in all six repeats. nnPU fails in both provinces (AUC 0.54--0.55). That Superior is the best-predicted of the three provinces agrees with the province-level success rates of \citet{Parsa2024}.

\subsection{Spatially blocked validation}\label{sec:res-spatial}

Holding out whole blocks widens the gap between the neural scorers and the tree-based and PU learners in Superior (Fig.~\ref{fig:spatial}; Table~\ref{tab:spatial}). DevNet's AP is 0.418, 0.343 and 0.252 with 50, 100 and 200~km blocks, and MLP-BCE's is similar (0.434, 0.346 and 0.233). RF's falls from 0.271 under random splits to 0.192, 0.168 and 0.151, and bagging-PU's to 0.148, 0.129 and 0.090. DevNet exceeds RF, bagging-PU and nnPU in all six repeats at every block size (Table~\ref{tab:spatial-boot}), by $+0.18$ to $+0.24$ against RF (nominal $p = 0.019$--0.027), $+0.25$ to $+0.33$ against bagging-PU ($p = 0.004$--0.018) and $+0.19$ to $+0.41$ against nnPU ($p \leq 0.007$). After Holm's correction over these nine comparisons, the differences from nnPU remain significant at every block size (adjusted $p \leq 0.040$) and those from bagging-PU at 50 and 200~km (0.025 and 0.040), whereas those from RF do not (0.074 at each block size), although DevNet has the higher AP in every one of the 18 repeat--block combinations. Capture-rate curves show where the AP differences arise (Fig.~\ref{fig:capture}). Under random splits DevNet and RF capture about half of the held-out occurrences in the top 1\% of the ranked pool (median 53\% and 50\%), and RF and bagging-PU capture all of them by the top 10\% (DevNet 93\%). Under 100~km blocks DevNet still captures 47\% in the top 1\% and 90\% in the top 10\%, against 43\% and 83\% for RF and 30\% and 83\% for bagging-PU. With 200~km blocks the top-10\% capture of every model falls to 63--70\%, and RF and bagging-PU (70\%) slightly exceed DevNet (63\%).

\subsection{Geological map classes and feature sets}\label{sec:res-featuresets}

How geology is encoded changes every model's skill more than the choice among DevNet, MLP-BCE and the tree-based learners does (Table~\ref{tab:featuresets}; Fig.~\ref{fig:featuresets}). \subsection{The deviation loss, label scarcity and contamination}\label{sec:res-loss}

With the full label set, DevNet and its supervised twin MLP-BCE are level on feature sets 1 and 3 (Table~\ref{tab:featuresets}), so the advantage over the tree-based and PU learners belongs to the network with its rank-normal inputs and background-heavy batches rather than to the deviation loss. Without point-level map classes (set 2) DevNet has the higher AP in 23 of the 24 repeat--protocol combinations, but by small margins ($\Delta$AP $-0.010$ to $+0.053$ on repeat-averaged ranks, $p \geq 0.13$). The loss makes a clear difference only when labels are fewest and the evidence weakest (Table~\ref{tab:scarcity}). With three training positives per fold and no point-level map classes (set 2), DevNet's AP is 0.081 under random splits and 0.070 under 100~km blocks, against 0.045 and 0.029 for MLP-BCE, higher in all six repeats ($\Delta$AP $+0.059$, $p = 0.046$, and $+0.072$, $p < 0.001$). With the map classes of set 3 the two are level at $k = 3$ (0.251 and 0.236; 0.154 and 0.142), and with set 1 MLP-BCE is ahead.

Hiding withheld positives in the training background rather than dropping them changed AP by 0.033 or less for every model, label count and feature subset (Table~\ref{tab:contamination}), and by at most 0.014 for DevNet. At this scale, undiscovered occurrences in the background are too few to separate the losses: background contamination is not where the semi-supervised formulation earns its keep.

\subsection{Tuned baselines and design selection}\label{sec:res-controls}

\subsection{How much of the score is location?}\label{sec:res-geography}

A random forest given only coordinates reaches an AP of 0.507 in Cordillera under random cross-validation, higher than any evidence-based model with feature set 3 (at most 0.470, HGB-tuned), and 0.249 in Grenville, about as high as the best of them (RF, 0.257; Table~\ref{tab:geography}). In these two provinces the random-split skill of the tree-based models can therefore be reproduced without any geology: training occurrences cluster, a random split leaves most held-out occurrences close to a training occurrence, and a flexible learner can exploit that proximity, through the coordinates themselves or through spatially smooth layers that act as surrogate coordinates. Under 100~km blocks the coordinate-only model collapses (0.052 and 0.075), as do the evidence-based models ($\leq 0.055$ and $\leq 0.009$).

Superior is the exception. Coordinates alone reach only 0.157 under random splits and 0.018 under blocking, far below every evidence-based model except nnPU, so the Superior scores reflect a signature in the evidence rather than location. With feature set 1, the smooth models (DevNet and an L2-regularised logistic regression) trail the tree-based models where location alone is predictive and lead in Superior, and DevNet uses the evidence better than the linear model in every province (Table~\ref{tab:geography}).

\subsection{Synthetic positives}\label{sec:res-augmentation}

The generators behave very differently (Table~\ref{tab:aug-diag}; Fig.~\ref{fig:aug-pca}). Jitter and SMOTE samples lie close to the real positives (median nearest-neighbour ratio 0.11 and 0.26) and almost all have a real positive as their nearest labelled neighbour (100\% and 96\%): they are near-copies of the training occurrences. WGAN-GP samples are as far from the real positives as the real positives are from one another (ratio 0.86), but for 45\% of them the nearest labelled point is a background point: with 15 training points the generator spreads probability mass into background-like parts of feature space. All three sets of samples receive high DevNet scores (median percentile 0.98).

Because the folds are identical with and without augmentation, the effect is measured as the paired, per-repeat difference in AP (feature set 1; Table~\ref{tab:augmentation}; Fig.~\ref{fig:aug-perf}). For DevNet no generator gives a consistent gain: the median per-repeat change in AP lies between $-0.016$ and $+0.040$, no configuration raises AP in more than four of the six repeats, and WGAN-GP lowers AUC slightly (0.940--0.943 against 0.951). For RF, SMOTE lowers AP (by 0.050 at $m = 10$, in all six repeats), and jitter and WGAN-GP at the highest multiplier raise it modestly (median $+0.025$ and $+0.041$, in five of six repeats, but with per-repeat differences ranging from $-0.06$ to $+0.22$). The best augmented RF (WGAN-GP $10\times$, AP 0.251) remains well below DevNet without augmentation (0.354). Under spatial blocking (Appendix~\ref{app:aug-spatial}, Table~\ref{tab:aug-spatial}) WGAN-GP raises RF's AP with 100~km blocks from 0.128 to 0.159--0.161 but not with 200~km blocks, and augmented RF stays far below unaugmented DevNet (0.302 and 0.341). For DevNet the per-repeat changes under blocking are similarly small (medians between $-0.006$ and $+0.041$); only jitter at $10\times$ with 200~km blocks raises AP in all six repeats, by a median of 0.010.

\subsection{Validation-protocol audit}\label{sec:res-audit}

Rebuilding the S2 parent--child augmentation and child-level random splitting of \citet{Parsa2024} on our data gives a test AUC of 0.998--1.000 in all three provinces and for both negative-sampling rules, with 2.5--97.5 percentile ranges no wider than 0.98--1.00 (Table~\ref{tab:audit}; Fig.~\ref{fig:audit}). In 97--99\% of the test positives, at least one sibling child cell of the same occurrence is in the training set. In feature space, siblings are closer to each other (median standardised distance 2.1--3.7) than occurrences are to their nearest other occurrence (4.1--6.7) and than background points are to their nearest background neighbour (4.5--4.7; Table~\ref{tab:audit-sim}), because the coarse deep-architecture layers take identical values in sibling cells and the fine-scale layers nearly so.

The honest reference within this balanced 70/30 design is one label per occurrence with random negatives. It gives a mean AUC of 0.946 in Superior, 0.772 in Cordillera and 0.624 in Grenville, each with a very wide range over the 50 splits (0.65--1.00, 0.58--0.92 and 0.26--0.99). The child-level split therefore inflates test AUC by about 0.05, 0.23 and 0.37 respectively, the inflation being largest where real skill is lowest, and it collapses the apparent uncertainty to almost nothing. Drawing negatives from the least similar 5\% of the ground inflates AUC independently of the split: without augmentation, cosine-dissimilar negatives raise mean AUC from 0.946 to 0.990 in Superior, from 0.772 to 0.993 in Cordillera and from 0.624 to 0.833 in Grenville. Keeping all children of one parent on the same side of the split removes most, but not all, of the augmentation effect (Superior 0.957, Cordillera 0.963, Grenville 0.829 with random negatives); the remainder reflects the fourfold larger training set and spatial dependence between neighbouring occurrences in different parent cells, which only spatial blocking removes.

\subsection{Model interpretation}\label{sec:res-shap}

With the rank-based direction rule, RF and DevNet agree on the sign of the effect for all ten of their jointly most important features in each province (Table~\ref{tab:shap}; Appendix~\ref{app:shap}). Read from the sign of the mean SHAP value instead, the two models would appear to disagree on many of these features, an artefact of the two explainers' different baselines (Section~\ref{sec:shap}).

The two models agree on directions but weight the evidence differently (Fig.~\ref{fig:shap}). In Superior, DevNet ranks five geological classes of the cell first: extrusive (greenstone) lithology, an age contact within the cell and alkalic intrusive lithology raise the score, and Archean era and tonalite lower it; it then ranks proximity to regional greenstone, Bouguer GLCM correlation (lower values more prospective) and LAB depth. RF ranks the horizontal-gradient magnitude of deep-source magnetics first (lower values more prospective), then LAB depth, proximity to regional greenstone and fault proximity, and gives the geological classes low ranks (20th--34th of 48). Both models score ground as more prospective where the LAB is shallower, close to greenstone belts and faults, where TMI is high, and farther from deep magnetic worms. In Cordillera both models treat Mesozoic terranes and ground far from high-grade metamorphic units as unfavourable and Proterozoic terranes as favourable. In Grenville both rank deep-source magnetics and distance to regional age contacts among their four most important features, but given the weak predictive skill there these attributions describe the signal the models attempted to use rather than a reliable control.

\subsection{Prospectivity maps and external checks}\label{sec:res-maps}

The production DevNet and RF maps (Fig.~\ref{fig:maps}) both concentrate their highest classes in the southern Superior Province, around the eastern-Abitibi cluster and across the Abitibi and Wawa subprovinces, but their rankings of the province are only moderately similar (Spearman correlation over the grid 0.37; DevNet and MLP-BCE 0.66). \section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{What the semi-supervised formulation adds}\label{sec:disc-devnet}

Three results bound the answer. First, in Superior DevNet ranks held-out occurrences better than RF, tuned RF, tuned gradient boosting, bagging-PU and nnPU in every repeat of every protocol, and its lead is largest when the test requires extrapolation: RF and bagging-PU, which learn a sharp decision boundary around the training positives, lose 30--60\% of their AP once the nearest training occurrence is 50--200~km away, whereas DevNet keeps most of its AP to 100~km. With 15 positives these differences are only marginally significant after correction for multiple comparisons, but they are consistent across all 18 repeat--block combinations, they survive a design search for the tree-based learners, and DevNet's own design choice carries little selection optimism.

Second, the advantage over the tree-based learners does not come from the deviation loss alone. DevNet's supervised twin, which shares its network, rank-normal inputs and background-heavy batches but labels the background 0, is level with it under every protocol on feature sets 1 and 3 when all labels are used, and only marginally behind it on set 2. What the tree-based learners lack is a smooth score over well-scaled inputs: their thresholds carve out the neighbourhood of each training positive, which is rewarded when held-out occurrences lie close by and penalised when they do not. The design of the network matters within this smooth model class (Appendix~\ref{app:sensitivity}, Table~\ref{tab:ablation}). Its score is a sum of a few ReLU ridges in the space of its inputs, so its resolution along each feature is set by how that feature is scaled; on z-scored, strongly skewed layers a handful of extreme values occupy most of the input range and the bulk of the ground is compressed into a narrow interval. Quantile-normal inputs give every feature a comparable, rank-based scale, the information that trees exploit through their thresholds, while keeping the score smooth, and background-heavy batches serve the premise of the deviation loss, whose inlier term is an average over the background rows of each batch.

Third, among formulations that treat the background as unlabelled, the deviation loss is the one that works here, and it helps over a supervised loss where it was designed to. nnPU, which differs from DevNet only in its risk, trails it by 0.19--0.41 in AP under blocking, fails in Cordillera and Grenville and with feature set 2, and depends on a class prior that is unknown in practice; bagging-PU trails it under every protocol. Against the supervised twin, the deviation loss adds skill when the inputs carry no point-level map classes, slightly with all labels and clearly when only three training positives remain, the regime of least information, where DevNet is ahead in all six repeats under both random and blocked splits. A plausible reading is that the inlier term, which pulls the scores of the whole background towards a common reference, regularises the score where a cross-entropy loss can only separate three points from the rest. It is not that the background hides deposits that a supervised loss would mislabel: at this scale, contaminating the background with withheld positives costs every model almost nothing.

The Cordilleran and Grenville results bound the conclusion further. Where positives are comparatively plentiful and clustered (Cordillera), or the geophysical signal is weak (Grenville), the tree-based learners and the supervised network are as good or better under random splits, a coordinate-only forest matches them, and no model transfers between blocks. DevNet is therefore not a universal replacement for discriminative baselines; its advantage is specific to the combination of very few positives, a shared control and the need to extrapolate.

\subsection{Geological map classes: evidence and lookup}\label{sec:disc-mapclasses}

The comparison of feature sets shows that the encoding of geology moves every model more than the choice of model does. Set 3, which gives each point the raw class of its H3 cell, raises DevNet's AP under random splits and 50--100~km blocks by 0.04--0.10 over the grouped classes of set 1, and withholding point-level classes altogether (set 2) costs DevNet, MLP-BCE and RF about half or more of their AP. Much of this comes from a single class: removing the alkalic-intrusion column lowers DevNet's blocked AP by over a third. Six of the 15 Superior occurrences lie on cells mapped as alkalic intrusive rock, which covers 0.5\% of the background; for these occurrences the map already marks the target. The age-contact flag of the containing cell partly does the same, because a Proterozoic complex mapped within Archean crust creates an age contact, and DevNet ranks it second.

Whether such lookup is acceptable depends on the use. For targeting within mapped complexes, or for ranking known complexes by their geophysical setting, a mapped alkaline intrusion is legitimate and strong evidence, and set 3 is the appropriate input. For finding complexes that the 1:5,000,000 map does not show, it is not, and the relevant skill is that of set 2: an AP of 0.08--0.13 under spatial blocking, 16--27 times the random-ranking value but well below what set 3 suggests. Comparisons between studies, and claims about how well a model ``discovers'' occurrences, should state which of the two is being measured. In both regimes DevNet is at or near the top under blocking, so the conclusions of Section~\ref{sec:disc-devnet} do not depend on the encoding, but its absolute skill does. The one exception is the deepest extrapolation: with 200~km blocks the grouped classes of set 1 give DevNet a higher AP (0.341) than the raw classes of set 3 (0.252), suggesting that the many lithology columns of set 3 add detail that transfers less well over the longest distances.

\subsection{Why the provinces differ}\label{sec:disc-provinces}

Grenville is the weakest of the three provinces for every model and Superior the strongest. The evidence layers are the same national compilations everywhere and almost complete (about 0.01\% of values missing in each province), so the contrast does not reflect data coverage. It follows instead from the label sets and from the geology they sample (Table~\ref{tab:region-diag}).

First, the effective number of independent occurrences is smaller than the count suggests, and smallest in Grenville. Merging occurrences within 10~km leaves 12 sites of 15 occurrences in Superior, 17 of 27 in Cordillera and 8 of 12 in Grenville, and in Cordillera and Grenville more than half of the occurrences share a site with another (median distance to the nearest other occurrence 7~km, against 31~km in Superior). Under a random split such neighbours fall on both sides, which is why coordinates alone score well in these two provinces (Section~\ref{sec:res-geography}) and why their skill collapses under blocking.

Second, only in Superior do distant occurrences share a geophysical signature. There the far-neighbour index has a median of 0.005, an order of magnitude below that of random pseudo-occurrences (0.049; 5th percentile 0.022): an occurrence resembles another occurrence more than 100~km away more closely than it resembles all but a few background points. In Cordillera (0.032 against 0.027) and Grenville (0.046 against 0.066) the index falls within the range of the random sets, so there is no shared signature for a model to transfer. The univariate evidence agrees: the median feature separates occurrences from background with $|\mathrm{AUC}-0.5| = 0.15$ in Superior but only 0.05 and 0.06 in Cordillera and Grenville, where three features and one feature respectively exceed 0.25.

Third, the Grenville label set mixes carbonatites of different ages and tectonic settings. Crevier is a late-Grenvillian alkaline--carbonatite intrusion of about 957~Ma \citep{Groulier2020}. St-Honoré, with Terres Rompues on the same complex, was emplaced at about 580~Ma in the Saguenay graben of the St.~Lawrence rift system \citep{Neron2018}. The Lavergne--Springer occurrences lie near Lake Nipissing at the western end of the Ottawa--Bonnech\`ere graben, and the Cantley, Quinnville and Templeton showings near Gatineau belong to the calcite-rich veins and dykes of the Grenville marble belts, whose magmatic origin has long been debated \citep{Moecher1997}. The far-neighbour index tracks this heterogeneity. The rift-related St-Honoré, Terres Rompues and Lavergne--Springer occurrences, about 700~km apart, resemble one another closely (indices 0.005--0.033), whereas Crevier (0.19), Megiscane Lake (0.28) and Sullivan Island (0.15) resemble no other occurrence. Age diversity alone is not the problem, since the Superior occurrences also span several magmatic episodes \citep{Rukhlov2010}. They were, however, all emplaced into the same stable cratonic lithosphere, whose margins and crustal-scale structures the present-day LAB, Moho and potential-field layers still record. The Grenville crust was reworked by the Grenvillian orogeny and by later rifting, so its present-day geophysics need not record a common setting for occurrences that formed in different ones. The mapped host terrane adds noise of its own: the twelve Grenville occurrences fall on Archean, Proterozoic and Paleozoic map units, because the bedrock map records reworked protoliths and the Paleozoic cover over St-Honoré rather than the carbonatites, and none of them lies on a cell mapped as alkalic intrusive rock, whereas about 5\% of the Grenville background does.

Missing or coarse geophysics therefore explains little of the contrast; the number, independence and geological coherence of the labels explain most of it. A model for Grenville would need either more occurrences of one carbonatite type or separate models for rift-related and orogenic types, which the present label set cannot support.

\subsection{Synthetic positives do not create information}\label{sec:disc-gan}

Synthetic positives are an attractive response to label scarcity, and GAN-generated labels have been recommended for exactly this commodity \citep{Parsa2025}. Our results counsel caution. A generator fitted to a dozen training positives has two options. It can stay close to them, in which case it adds near-duplicates that carry no new information (jitter and SMOTE, nearest-neighbour ratios of 0.1--0.3); or it can spread out, in which case it must guess where unseen occurrences lie and, having no information about that, places much of its mass in background-like feature space (WGAN-GP, 45\% of samples nearest to a background point). Neither option supplies the information that is actually missing, namely what undiscovered occurrences look like. The small and inconsistent gains for RF are better explained as smoothing: synthetic points around and between the training positives make more trees vote for nearby held-out points, which relieves RF's degenerate surface of exactly-zero probabilities rather than teaching it new geology. DevNet, whose loss already produces smooth scores, gains nothing consistent from the same points. Consistent with this reading, RF's gain from WGAN-GP shrinks as the held-out blocks grow and vanishes at 200~km. Where synthetic positives are used, they must be generated inside each training fold from that fold's positives only, and performance must be measured on real held-out occurrences; otherwise a synthetic copy of a training occurrence in the test set reproduces the sibling problem of Section~\ref{sec:res-audit} in another form.

\subsection{The validation protocol decides many reported differences}\label{sec:disc-protocol}

Four of our findings concern evaluation rather than models. First, with tens of positives a single cross-validation split gives a noisy estimate: re-splitting the same Superior data moves each model's AP over a range of 0.07--0.17 (Table~\ref{tab:benchmark}), as large as many of the differences between models. Second, random splits overstate skill wherever occurrences cluster, and the size of the overstatement differs between models, so random splits can also reverse a model ranking (Section~\ref{sec:res-spatial}). Third, splitting augmented child cells at random yields near-perfect test AUC on these data whatever the negatives, because almost every test positive has a sibling, sharing the coarse deep-architecture values exactly and the fine-scale values nearly, on the training side. In this design the held-out test asks the model to recognise a location it has already seen, and the comparison of training and test AUC, which \citet{Parsa2024} used to argue that their models were not over-fitted, becomes uninformative because the test set is not independent of the training set. Selecting negatives from the least similar 5\% of the ground adds a second, independent source of optimism: it removes from evaluation exactly the ground on which a prospectivity model is hardest to get right. Fourth, the geological encoding can supply a large share of the reported skill by marking known complexes (Section~\ref{sec:disc-mapclasses}), which no split design removes.

We stress what the audit does and does not show. It uses our features, regions and models, not those of \citet{Parsa2024}, and it does not establish what their numbers would have been under a grouped split. It does show that, on comparable Canadian carbonatite data, the published design produces test AUCs close to 1 whether the underlying skill is high (Superior) or weak (Grenville, honest AUC 0.62), and that the reported 0.91--0.96 cannot be taken as evidence of generalisation to unexplored ground until the augmented children of one occurrence are kept on one side of the split. \citet{Parsa2025} describe the same stratified random 70/30 split for a workflow that adds GAN-generated labels; their description does not state whether synthetic labels derived from an original label were kept on the same side of the split as that label. If they were not, the caveat applies with greater force. For prospectivity studies under extreme label scarcity we therefore recommend (i) grouping every augmented or near-duplicate label with its source occurrence before any split, (ii) drawing evaluation negatives at random from the domain rather than from the ground least similar to the positives, (iii) reporting AP with intervals over repeated splits rather than a single AUC, (iv) adding a spatially blocked validation in which training background is purged from held-out blocks, (v) reporting a geography-only baseline, so that readers can see how much of a score is location, and (vi) reporting results with and without point-level map classes, so that readers can see how much of a score is map lookup.

\subsection{Geological interpretation}\label{sec:disc-geology}

The drivers on which RF and DevNet agree in Superior describe a coherent setting. Shallower LAB, together with the strong ranking of deep-source magnetic gradients, points to the thinner lithosphere at the edges of the thick Superior root, where small-degree melting of metasomatised mantle and access along lithosphere-scale structures are most likely \citep{Griffin2013,Groves2021,Hoggard2020}; a mantle conductor beneath the southern Abitibi is consistent with such metasomatism \citep{Snyder2021}. Fault proximity and high magnetic intensity capture the structures that focus ascent and the magnetite-bearing intrusions themselves \citep{Thomas2016}. The negative effect of proximity to deep magnetic worms is less intuitive; it may reflect the concentration of deep magnetic contacts along terrane boundaries that are not themselves carbonatite hosts, and should not be over-interpreted.

The strong positive effect of greenstone lithology and of proximity to regional greenstone units needs more care. Eight of the 15 occurrences belong to the eastern-Abitibi cluster in a greenstone-dominated subprovince, so the association may partly record where the largest cluster lies rather than a control shared by the dispersed occurrences; the dispersed occurrences that DevNet ranks lowest under blocking, Kapuskasing and Nemegosenda Lake, lie in the high-grade and alkaline terrain of the KSZ rather than in a greenstone belt. Consistent with this, the set-3 DevNet map follows the KSZ less clearly than the set-1 map did, while the untrained Clay-Howells resource on the KSZ still ranks at its 97th percentile. A model whose top classes are tied to one greenstone-hosted cluster is useful for ranking ground of that type, but it should not be expected to recognise alkaline complexes in other settings as well.

\subsection{Limitations}\label{sec:limitations}

Fifteen positives allow only weak statistical statements: our significant results are those that are consistent across repeats, block sizes and comparisons, and several of them do not survive correction for multiple comparisons on their own. DevNet's input representation and batch composition were chosen with diagnostic experiments on these three provinces; the nested selection on feature set 1 shows little optimism, but confirmation on an independent province is needed, and the Churchill Province, the obvious candidate, holds too few carbonatite-hosted occurrences after deduplication for a meaningful test. The feature sets were defined in sequence, sets 2 and 3 after set 1 had been evaluated; set 3 raised the AP of every model except nnPU under random splits and 50--100~km blocks but lowered DevNet's, MLP-BCE's and bagging-PU's with 200~km blocks, so it is not uniformly better. The evaluation background may contain undiscovered occurrences, so all metrics are lower bounds. Radiometric data could not be used because of incomplete coverage, and whole-rock geochemistry is too sparse to serve as an input. Eleven gravity and magnetic layers are proprietary Getech products; equivalent derivatives can be computed from the public parent grids, but results will differ somewhat. The provenance of the fault compilation should be confirmed against the original data release before publication. The occurrence-class information (Deposit/Prospect, Showing, Occurrence) was not used for weighting; with 3--8 occurrences per class, confidence-weighted training could not be evaluated reliably. Finally, maps are per-region percentiles and are not calibrated probabilities.

\section{Conclusions}\label{sec:conclusions}

For regional carbonatite-hosted REE prospectivity mapping with 12--27 occurrences per province:
\begin{enumerate}
\item In the Superior Province a semi-supervised Deviation Network ranks held-out occurrences better than Random Forest, tuned Random Forest and gradient boosting, bagging-PU and nnPU in every repeat under random and 50--200~km spatially blocked cross-validation (AP 0.45 and 0.42--0.25 against 0.27 and 0.19--0.15 for RF); the margin is consistent but only marginally significant after correction for multiple comparisons.
\item The advantage over the tree-based learners belongs to a smooth neural score over rank-normal inputs with background-heavy batches: a supervised twin network with the same design is level with DevNet. The deviation loss itself adds skill over the supervised loss only in the regime of least information, without point-level map classes and most clearly with three training positives, and it clearly outperforms the other unlabelled-background formulations, nnPU and bagging-PU.
\item How geology is encoded moves every model more than the choice of model. A single mapped alkalic-intrusion class, covering 6 of 15 Superior occurrences and 0.5\% of the background, carries over a third of DevNet's blocked AP, and without point-level map classes most models lose half or more of their skill. Prospectivity studies should report results with and without such classes.
\item In the Cordilleran Orogen and the Grenville Province the tree-based learners and the supervised network lead under random splits, a forest given only coordinates matches them, and no model transfers between blocks; the number, independence and geological coherence of the labels, not data coverage, explain the contrast.
\item Synthetic positives from jitter, SMOTE or a tabular WGAN-GP, generated strictly within training folds, gave neither DevNet nor RF a consistent gain; splitting augmented child cells of one occurrence between training and test sets yields near-perfect test AUC irrespective of skill.
\item RF and DevNet agree on the direction of all 30 leading drivers across the three provinces. DevNet's Superior map is more stable than RF's when occurrences are left out, places the untrained Clay-Howells Fe--REE--Nb resource at its 97th percentile, and favours greenstone-hosted settings like the dominant Abitibi cluster.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Ablation of DevNet design choices}\label{app:sensitivity}

DevNet's two design choices (Section~\ref{sec:models}) were settled with diagnostic experiments on feature set 1 in the three provinces of this study, by changing one element at a time from a configuration with z-scored inputs and label-balanced batches of 16 + 16 rows. Each configuration in Table~\ref{tab:ablation} was fixed before it was run and was not tuned afterwards, and all are evaluated with the folds, protocols and seeds of the main results. In Superior, quantile inputs alone raise AP from 0.209--0.272 to 0.278--0.328 across protocols, and background-heavy batches add a smaller, less consistent gain on top of them. Training for longer, by contrast, repeats the dozen positives and pushes the network towards memorising their neighbourhoods: with z-scored inputs, ten times more steps raised random-split AP but lowered spatial transfer (Superior, 100~km blocks: 0.190 against 0.209). The nested early-stopping configuration chooses the number of steps (100--3,000, cosine schedule over 3,000 steps) inside each outer training fold by a 100~km spatial cross-validation (three inner folds, two inner restarts, background purged from inner validation blocks), so the test fold is never used for the choice. Table~\ref{tab:margin} gives the sensitivity of the final configuration to the deviation margin: changing $a$ within $\{2, 3, 5, 8\}$ moved Superior AP by less than 0.01, while in Cordillera and Grenville smaller margins gave somewhat higher random-split AP.
